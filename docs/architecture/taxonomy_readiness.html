Procedural-error AutoQC: which error kinds are ready to deploy

Evidence from two test sets, measured 24–28 Sep 2026. Every count on this page comes from the pipeline's stored results scored against labels; scenario explanations are written from the same results.
Read these numbers as direction, not certification. Each kind has between 1 and 19 labelled cases, so the 95 % intervals are wide. Both sets were measured with the current pipeline; the previous version's synthetic results are shown for comparison in each section. A kind marked ready means ready to flag for a human, not to act without one.

Early termination Ready for assisted QC

What it is. The procedure is not carried through to its mandatory final step, so the goal state is never reached. How it is checked. One judge reads the narration and checks whether the final step was performed and the goal state holds at the end. It does not care how the video file ends.

Recommended deployment: Flag automatically; a reviewer confirms before any action. Watch brief final actions.

False alarm Synthetic · eb7d161e_ins01

The person does stand up (the procedure's final step, about one second) in the last seconds. Reported that the video ends while seated.

Why: A very short final action at the very end is easy to miss at the sampling rate used.

Why it works or is hard

  • The evidence is the end state, which is usually clear: the last unit of work visibly never happens.
  • It relies on how the narration describes the last seconds. In one synthetic recording the narration reported the final step as done (it read a pressing motion as 'reposition the book'), so the judge correctly found no termination and reported a skipped step instead.
  • Failure mode seen: a very short final action (a one-second 'stand up') in the last seconds of the video is missed, and a termination is reported.

What would fix it: Sample the final seconds densely; treat body movements (stand up, sit down) as weak final steps; ask the narrator to describe the last seconds explicitly.

Off-procedure addition Ready for assisted QC

What it is. An action that belongs to no step of the procedure and serves none (another activity, an unrelated object). How it is checked. The judge looks at narrated actions that match no step and asks whether they prepare, support or tidy up after any step. Only actions that serve no step are reported.

Recommended deployment: Flag automatically; a reviewer confirms. Actions that resemble a listed step can slip through.

Why it works or is hard

  • Spliced footage from another activity is obvious to the model (different room, different objects), and it is reported with the right time window.
  • An inserted action that resembles a listed step (a 'sit down' clip in a task that has sitting in it) is matched to that step and not reported.
  • On real recordings, ordinary preparation that the procedure does not spell out (fetching, washing, halving) produced 10 false alarms until the definition said that anything serving a step is not an addition; now none.

What would fix it: Keep the service-action rule; report 'real but unlisted' actions to the procedure owner as possible missing steps; flag narrated actions whose scene differs from the rest of the video.

Omission Assisted QC, known blind spot

What it is. A required step is never performed although the procedure continued past it. How it is checked. The judge checks, step by step, whether the narration performs it; if not, whether a step that needs it was performed anyway.

Recommended deployment: Flag automatically; a reviewer confirms. Treat omissions of look-alike repeated steps (e.g. one of three microwave steps) as unreliable until the identity convention is fixed.

Missed Synthetic · 52864eed_omit04

Synthetic cut: the unit 'place the stamp pad and its lid on the table' was removed, but the pad is on the table in every later frame.

Why: A limitation of the synthetic test: re-cutting leaves the step's effect visible, so the video shows no missing step.

Why it works or is hard

  • Works when a step's result never appears and later steps that need it are done anyway (mayonnaise never reaches the bowl, avocado never sliced).
  • Fails on look-alike repeated steps: the recipe has three 'microwave the ramekin' steps. Which step an action counts as depends on a convention (by position in the sequence, as the annotators use, or by content, as the model does). The prompts do not state one yet.
  • Synthetic omissions are a weak test: cutting a clip out leaves the step's effect visible afterwards, so there is often nothing in the video to detect; when the model does react, it blames a neighbouring step.

What would fix it: List look-alike steps and what distinguishes them in the graph context; let the narrator give all candidate steps for an ambiguous action; state the identity convention (needs a decision); one short text call assigns identities before the judges.

Redundant repetition Not ready, narration gap

What it is. A step allowed once is performed again after it was already complete, with no mistake in between. How it is checked. The judge compares repeated occurrences of a step and asks whether the first one had already achieved its result.

Recommended deployment: Keep off. The current narration merges a repeated step into one entry, so the judge never sees the repeat. Fixable in the narration rules; re-test after.

Missed Synthetic · 1a08eacf_rep03

The nozzle-insertion clip was duplicated. The narration lists the insertion once; the repeat judge reports every step performed exactly once. (The previous version found this one, at review level.)

Why: The narration merges an exact repeat into one entry, so the judge never sees it.

Missed Synthetic · ade37404_rep04

A 26-second cord-coiling clip was duplicated; the narration describes one coiling. (Also found by the previous version, at review level.)

Missed Synthetic · e2db3119_rep13

A 1.2-second placement was duplicated; it reads as one continuous motion.

Why: Short repeats are nearly invisible at the sampling rate.

Why it works or is hard

  • The narration rules tell the narrator to merge exact repeats into one entry with a count. That rule was meant for wipe strokes and stirring passes, but a duplicated step is exactly such a repeat, so it disappears before the judge sees it.
  • The previous version, which asked the video model directly about repeats, found 2 of 3, both only at human-review level.
  • Beyond that, the line between a redundant repeat and a correction depends on whether the first attempt had failed, which is an outcome judgement.

What would fix it: Change the narration rule: a whole step performed again is always a separate entry (only strokes within one action are merged). Show the adjudicator both occurrences (done). Re-test on real repeats.

Misordering (wrong order) Not ready

What it is. A step is performed before a step it requires, and the required step is done later. How it is checked. The judge walks the narration in time order against the dependency graph (unrelated branches may be done in any order).

Recommended deployment: Keep off in production; keep measuring.

Missed Real (CaptainCook4D) · cc4d_1_32

Labelled order error: the lettuce was removed, the egg placed, then the lettuce put on top of the egg.

Why: The wrong order is inside one assembly (which layer first), which the step list describes only implicitly.

False alarm Real (CaptainCook4D) · cc4d_1_10

Reported 'stirred before microwaving'. Physically true, but the annotators treat the beating as the stir step and the first microwave step as skipped.

Why: Same look-alike identity problem as omission; misordering inherits every step-identity error.

Why it works or is hard

  • The narrator assigns step ids with the procedure in view and tends to follow the written order, so swapped steps can come out in the expected order and the judge sees nothing wrong.
  • It inherits every step-identity error: if one action is assigned to the wrong look-alike step, an order violation appears (or disappears).
  • Wrong order inside one assembly (which layer goes first) is only implicit in the step list; synthetic swaps are physically impossible for state-changing steps.

What would fix it: Narrate with the objects list but without the step order, and match steps in a separate text pass, so the narration cannot follow the script; plus the identity fix above.

Wrong tool / object / ingredient Not ready, gap is fixable

What it is. A step is performed with a different tool, object or ingredient than specified. How it is checked. The judge compares the objects the narration names for each step with the objects the step specifies.

Recommended deployment: Keep off; the detector sees several of these events but files them under another kind. Fix the definitions, then re-test.

Labelled: a bowl was used instead of the ramekin. The narration even calls it a 'ceramic bowl', but the judge did not treat it as a different object.

Why: Near-synonym objects: nothing in the procedure says a bowl is not acceptable.

Missed Real (CaptainCook4D) · cc4d_1_34

Labelled: stirred with a knife.

Why: The recipe never names the stirring tool, so there is nothing to compare against.

Why it works or is hard

  • The detector does see several of these events, but the definitions of 'wrong tool' and 'wrong technique' overlap: tearing a muffin by hand instead of cutting it with a knife was filed as a parameter (technique) deviation.
  • Near-synonym objects pass as close enough: a bowl used instead of a ramekin was even named in the narration, but nothing in the procedure says a bowl is not acceptable.
  • Some errors concern tools the step text never names (stirring with a knife, chopping without a board); a wrong tool can only be judged against a specified one.

What would fix it: Define the boundary with parameter deviation (tool or object identity takes precedence); add excluded substitutes to objects (a bowl is not a ramekin); name the tools a step requires when the client's procedure does.

Step parameter deviation Not ready

What it is. The right step with the wrong quantity, duration, setting or technique. How it is checked. The judge compares quantities, durations and settings the narration reports with those the step text specifies.

Recommended deployment: Keep off except for counts that are clearly visible (number of eggs, scoops); keep measuring.

Why it works or is hard

  • Of the 19 labelled deviations, most are technique judgements ('stirred too rapidly', 'cracked the egg inappropriately') with no stated threshold, or small quantities (a teaspoon versus a tablespoon) that are hard to see from a head camera at 360p.
  • Timing errors need the microwave display or the door-closed time, which the narration does not measure reliably.
  • What works: counts that are plainly visible (two eggs instead of one, two scoops instead of one) and clear durations.

What would fix it: Split into measurable parameters (counts, visible durations, settings shown on a display) and subjective technique; deploy only the measurable part first; technique needs client-defined thresholds.

How this was measured

Synthetic set. 15 re-cut Lightwheel recordings, 3 per injected kind, no clean recordings. Errors were created by cutting, duplicating, swapping or splicing clips of real recordings. Measured with the current pipeline (narrate the video, judge each error kind against the task graph, adjudicator with frames from annotator timestamps).

Real set. 10 cooking recordings (5.7 to 18.5 min) with real, human-annotated errors, 2 recipes with published step-dependency graphs, 4 recordings labelled clean. Measured with the current pipeline (narrate the video, judge each error kind against the recipe graph, adjudicator with frames from annotator timestamps). Clean recordings falsely flagged: 0 of 4.

Scoring. A finding counts as caught when its error kind and step match a label. Early termination is matched by kind (an episode terminates once). Off-procedure additions have no step, so they are matched by time overlap. Wrong-tool and parameter labels exist only in the real set.

Cost and speed (current pipeline). Real set: about $0.31 per 11-minute recording ($0.27 narration and judges, $0.04 adjudication); narration about 56 s per recording (median), adjudication about 22 s. Synthetic set (about 1-minute recordings): $0.10 per recording and 52 s end to end (median; 95th percentile 109 s): narration 27 s, the seven judges in parallel 12 s, adjudication 21 s.

What is not tested yet. Correction loops; wrong tool and parameter deviation on the synthetic set (they cannot be made by re-cutting video); early termination, off-procedure additions and repeats on real recordings (the real set has none labelled); annotator mistakes (planned separately).
